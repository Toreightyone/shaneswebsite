*Originally submitted to the Toronto Star on December 5, 2024*

[The TTC announced on Wednesday that some fare inspection officers will begin to do their duties in plain clothes](https://www.thestar.com/news/gta/creepy-ttc-is-rolling-out-plainclothes-inspectors-to-fight-fare-evasion-critics-arent-impressed/article_6ec30ca4-b26a-11ef-bd1e-4b5c7141f90b.html), without the big and bold black vest and without an obvious presence. You could be sitting on a streetcar and someone nondescript may end up asking you to fork over your debit card, credit card, phone, or PRESTO card. Sure, they could show a badge or some sort of identification, but how can you truly trust that someone is who they say they are, especially at night, and especially if you are a woman or of a marginalised identity.

The intent is clearly to scare you into paying; to stoke fear that any of your fellow passengers could potentially give you a \$425 fine, one that does not even go to the TTC.

It is one of many gripes I have with fare inspection. Its existence is necessary and justified given the millions the TTC lose to non-payment. However, certain tactics make fare-paying customers feel like criminals just for riding with them.

It is the actual use of kettling at streetcar terminals to funnel the potentially two hundred aboard a streetcar into a single file line so a few inspectors can check payment, delaying riders' arrival to work or missing a transfer. It is the fare inspectors inside subway stations behind the fare gates checking those who had just evidently tapped to get into the station. It is students who are identified as having already paid \$128.15 for the post-secondary monthly pass getting targeted more often to prove they are a student.

There is not even consistent or clear messaging from TTC officials or on-the-ground-staff. If someone from Malvern commutes downtown, and it takes more than their two-hour transfer, would they get a ticket if they were inspected? [The TTC website says no, while executive-level staff at a TTC board meeting earlier in July says there is a 20-minute grace period.](https://stevemunro.ca/2024/07/17/ttc-contemplates-fare-evasion/) If you were let in by a station collector on discretion, a fare inspector could still give you a ticket.

On buses and at subway stations, staff are readily available; however, on streetcars the driver has their own cab and it is not immediately apparent that one is allowed to bother them. I have seen tourists, some non-English speaking, get incredibly confused over how to navigate the constantly changing detours and how to pay their fare – because most want to, but to create an environment where one must figure it out before a fare inspector gets them, is not a welcoming one, especially for a global city soon hosting a World Cup. What is truly needed is more customer service, someone you can freely go to ask questions about paying your fare, planning your trip, or to report a safety concern without fear of a Provincial Offence Notice.

Riders get scared, anxious, or nervous anytime they see a fare inspector, even if they paid their fare. There are proven histories of fare inspections on transit agencies across the continent targeting marginalised and racialised populations more often than White, male passengers. This is not something that the TTC should be in the business of perpetuating and plain-clothes officers demanding proof of payment [under a vague use-of-force policy](https://www.thestar.com/news/gta/ttc-fare-inspectors-special-constables-to-wear-body-cameras-in-pilot-project/article_44c12c38-f9b5-11ee-af3f-733391feb4de.html) will not ameliorate this.

In Amsterdam, there are staffed kiosks on-board every tram that provide maps and information and allows paying your fare with irregular methods like cash. In Stockholm, all trams have a staff member available for questions that go up and down the vehicle with a payment machine. If one refuses, that is when enforcement will be called. Both are non-intrusive, helps people who want to pay to be able to do so, boost fare compliance, and overall improves the experience of riders, something the TTC could strive for.
