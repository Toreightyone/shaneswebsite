Letting Toronto manage its own roads would do more for traffic than Doug Ford's bike lane ban ever could

Doug Ford's provincial government has signalled his intention to "solve" traffic: a rule purely based in ideology that would [prohibit new bike lanes in cases where lanes for vehicles would be removed](https://www.thestar.com/politics/provincial/ford-government-to-restrict-new-bike-lanes-in-bid-to-ease-gridlock/article_8a6870c0-7755-11ef-b901-4f02b8c46b12.html). This overreach into city planning is indicative of how provincial guidelines, for years, have stunted the extent to which cities can implement measures to ameliorate traffic.

Traffic is a simple geometry problem. Toronto's population of almost three million people cannot each get a 4.5-metre-long vehicle and expect to fit on our existing road network; it is akin to stuffing the square peg into a round hole. A single crush-loaded Dufferin, Sheppard, Jane, or Markham Road bus could create a kilometre-long line of cars otherwise. If all [15,750 Torontonians who reported biking to work on the 2021 census](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&GENDERlist=1,2,3&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0&DGUIDlist=2021A00053520005&SearchText=toronto) drove, 70.8 km/day of traffic would be added to our roads.

This plan essentially relegates new bike lanes to road peripheries, effectively banning new bike lanes downtown as there is not enough space to put them off-road without removing street trees or shrinking sidewalk width. Bike lanes are proven to not only increase the capacity of roads, but also greatly reduce injury and death. By ensuring that some of the deadliest roads in the province may never have safe bike infrastructure, cyclists will be forced to ride in mixed traffic with two-tonne steel boxes travelling at double or triple the speed, leading to road rage, violence, and death.

The more sensible alternative is opening up the Highway Traffic Act and the Planning Act to more interpretations from municipalities – interpretations based on facts, data, and specificities to the respective city – cities' staffs could access a wide range of options to fixing traffic. These could range from redesigning signals, signs, and roads to introducing innovative rules, regulations, and enforcement measures from abroad that have proven to work. It should not take months to years to wait for provincial approval to use the city's own cameras to catch drivers blowing past streetcar doors, blocking the intersection, speeding, or running red lights. The two acts may be sufficient for Sudbury or Timiskaming, but Toronto, being as dense as it is, has problems that cannot be addressed by boilerplate guidelines. The work done at the municipal level to make our commutes faster is seemingly not done with effectiveness in mind, but rather what would get through the province in the shortest amount of time, and this has led to the muted response to traffic we get from the city. Walking on nails has meant [our proposed solution for congestion is increasing fines, knowing police enforcement is few and far between, levies on construction companies, and politely asking for the ability to use our own cameras to enforce rules.](https://www.thestar.com/news/gta/do-it-faster-citys-new-plans-to-tackle-torontos-terrible-congestion-include-new-levy-for/article_7d4c599e-7483-11ef-98ed-a70ff15684a7.html)

When the province of Quebec allowed right turns on red lights back in 2003, [they let Montreal decide to keep the restriction](https://www.mtlblog.com/montreal/why-montreal-island-has-no-right-turn-on-red) – a decision based on a plethora of evidence that allowing such turns results in more collisions with pedestrians. Meanwhile in cities like Amsterdam or Copenhagen, the levels of bike, pedestrian, and transit infrastructure available takes so many cars off the road that it makes driving equally viable despite the cities' densities.

If Doug Ford really wanted to help Toronto's congestion, he could inject funding into the TTC to improve service and bus, streetcar, and subway frequency. Perhaps he could expedite fixing the slow zones that have plagued the subway system for over a year as he did to speed up Gardiner Expressway reconstruction. He could reverse course and add congestion pricing to the Gardiner and the Don Valley Parkway. However, all this appears to be is a continuation of his fight as city councillor and mayoral candidate against an imaginary "war on cars" and will not address the root causes of traffic.

_Shane Gates is an undergraduate student at the University of Toronto majoring in urban planning._
