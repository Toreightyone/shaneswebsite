*Originally submitted to the Toronto Star on April 15, 2026*

****

I am graduating this year with a Bachelor of Arts in Human Geography with a focus in Urban Planning. In all my research, assignments, and trips abroad, I studied urban spaces that people actually want to travel to, not just through; spaces that encourage social, cultural, and economic production; and spaces that builds community. Amsterdam, Vienna, Tokyo, Hanoi, New York City, even Montreal, Vancouver, and Kitchener-Waterloo. These cities identified areas where pedestrians overwhelmingly outnumber drivers and decided it makes zero sense from many perspectives to have a majority of the space be allocated to transporting and storing cars, when that space could instead be used to hold so many more pedestrians and cyclists that shop, enjoy the vibes, interact with each other, and create connections. These spaces are usually outfitted with food stalls or food trucks, patios, interactive street furniture, stages, and greenery among many other pleasant features.

I looked at so many successful pedestrianization projects feeling a sense of FOMO, and it is because Toronto has none, beyond U of T, TMU, and the small street next to St. Lawrence Market sometimes. That will hopefully change on April 30, when the Toronto East York Community Council will vote on closing Church Street from Wellesley to Alexander Streets each summer. It is a no brainer. When hundreds on weekdays and thousands on weekends crowd the very narrow sidewalk, trying their best to contribute to the local economy, it seems like the pro-business move would be to facilitate that.

We all swoon over Times Square in New York City, but pre-2009, the southern leg of Broadway Avenue passed through the now car-free space. Pedestrians outnumbered cars by four and a half times, but 89% of the road space was allocated towards cars while the remaining 11% was shared amongst vendors, furniture, fire hydrants, and pedestrians. After its closure from 45<sup>th</sup> Street to 47<sup>th</sup> Street, pedestrian injuries dropped 35% and collisions dropped 63%, both on the entire Avenue. Real estate values of Times Square increased from USD\$2 billion in 1992 to USD\$5.6 billion in 2012. However, pertinently, users of Times Square reported a higher satisfaction of the space, residents were more inclined to shop there, and nearby office workers reported going out to eat more often.

In Copenhagen, Nørrebrogade shut down to the 17,000 cars per day that used the road in favour of a pedestrian-priority market street. Car traffic in the surrounding area plummeted by 41%, noise was reduced by 3.5 decibels, 26% more cyclists used the street, and 8,300 fewer tonnes of carbon dioxide were released into the atmosphere. 67% of survey respondents called for the project to be permanent.

Toronto had some semblance of this with community streets and ActiveTO during the pandemic; I remember tangoing for the first time in the middle of Yorkville Avenue – something the city would never dare repeat again. You could look at bike lane installations along [Bloor and Danforth – when from 2015 to 2017](https://www.tcat.ca/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Bloor-Economic-Impact-Study-Full-Report-2019-09-03.pdf), visitors increased, spending increased, and vacancy rates did not change – or summer street festivals; the benefits to businesses could last beyond just a weekend. It is obvious that this portion of Church Street should be car-free; Montreal has done it along a much larger portion of roadway every summer for years and [is looking at making it year-round by 2030](https://ici.radio-canada.ca/nouvelle/2187506/rue-sainte-catherine-est-travaux-pietonnisation-ville-montreal). Pedestrianization has been done, both here and internationally, but every opportunity is such a fight. I am embarrassed at the prospect of World Cup fans seeing how we treat our tourist destinations-cum-parking lots: Yonge and Dundas Streets/Sankofa Square, Kensington Market, Yorkville, High Park, Chinatown. Thank goodness the Gay Village can soon be removed from this list, but it is time to play catch up with best practices abroad to create a more liveable Toronto.
