[Halloween is statistically the most dangerous day of the year for children](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2022/10/31/23427256/halloween-children-car-deaths-pedestrian-safety). According to [data from the Toronto Police](https://data.tps.ca/datasets/a96252bf61b84fc68c3926bb7485970e_0/explore), between 2008 and 2016 on October 31sts, six children under fourteen years of age were struck by drivers (figure 1). ["The children weren't doing anything wrong. They were trick-or-treaters who were out having a good time", said Const. Clint Stibbe](https://globalnews.ca/news/3037523/man-charged-after-three-trick-or-treaters-struck-by-vehicle-in-toronto/) after a driver struck three 13–14-year-olds at Lawrence and Don Mills in 2016.

Trick-or-treating is ultimately a ritual of making connections between neighbours, often for the only time in a particular year. It should not be a dangerous activity but rather facilitated. However. we created our neighbourhoods to have a hostile moat running down its centre and then teach children to treat it as such, putting the onus on the child to not get hit by a driver; but we then have one night where the neighbourhoods' children are suddenly invited and encouraged to traverse the space? Of course, these are conditions for serious injury or death year-round, but frequency peaks particularly on Halloween when its darker earlier and there are more chances for collisions to occur.

Figure Table of driver-pedestrian collisions on October 31st between 2007 and 2023. One child was hit in 2008 at Valermo/Browns Line, one in 2013 at Danforth/Barrington, one in 2015 at Sheppard/Massie, three in 2016 at Lawrence/Don Mills.

I remember as a kid, my school talked extensively about trick-or-treating safety. They would say to avoid dark coloured costumes (lame) and to look both ways before crossing. They offered adhesive reflectors you can put on your costume (lamer), keychain flashlights, and a whistle. It would have been so much simpler if the burden of safety did not fall on a ten-year-old, but instead on the city to create a safe environment to go door-to-door, like allowing communities to organise block parties, only permitting local vehicle access to some neighbourhood streets, or by closing streets entirely.

On my Bluesky feed recently were posts from Mayors Zohran Mamdani and Katie Wilson of New York City and Seattle respectively advertising their cities' initiatives implementing these very solutions ("Trick-or-Streets").

So, I looked up what it would take if a Toronto neighbourhood wanted to set up a block party of sorts or just wanted to limit access to their road so trick-or-treating can be done safely. (1) \$35.20 plus tax for the application, (2) \$117.00 plus tax for the permit, (3) a \$2,000,000 insurance policy, (4) eight weeks notice, (5) security guards/police manning the closure, (6) a waste management plan which may require hiring a waste collection contractor, (7) a site plan that outlines "traffic control and public safety", (8) paying out the parking meters, and (9) paying for or arranging signage and blockades from a contractor. A laundry list of things that community members may not have time or money to organise, so they just choose not to. An "irresolvable" objection would also send the application to the respective community council to decide. To submit the application, you must deliver the whole package in person to either Metro Hall, the Etobicoke Civic Centre, the North York Civic Centre, or the Scarborough Civic Centre, depending on the ward. This is the same process that the Taste of the Danforth, the Toronto International Film Festival, or the 42-kilometre Toronto Waterfront Marathon goes through, but for some reason equally applies to a neighbourhood wanting to close a block for a couple hours; even if there are no activities planned and the intention is purely for children's safety.

New York City and Seattle on the other hand frankly puts us to shame in simplifying this process so that neighbourhoods can create those safe conditions so children, and the community at large, can actually enjoy themselves.

I decided to go through the process for a block party permit with the NYC Event Permits department, stopping short of actually submitting one. You make an account, select a date sixty days in advance, choose a nine-hour window for the event to be held, answer a questionnaire, upload a site plan, and then pay \$20. After payment, it looks like it gets sent to staff and they work with you to make it work. It took less than ten minutes. [The city is hosting five of their own car-free Halloween events across the five boroughs, accompanied by 146 community-led events city-wide.](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/pedestrians/trickorstreets.shtml)

[Seattle's is somehow even easier, is completely free, and can be done with as little as five days' notice](https://sdotblog.seattle.gov/2026/09/17/host-a-trick-or-streets-celebration-apply-by-october-20/), encompassing both Halloween and _Día de los Muertos_. For a closure up to three blocks, it is just a short form you fill out. Staff will review it, and then all you need to do is inform your neighbours via mailbox placard, print out the signs, blockade the street using household items, and have an adult stand at the entrances/exits. You can quite literally print out a sign, tape it to your garbage bin, wheel it onto the road, and that is sufficient for marking the closure.

It is baffling how unnecessarily difficult the process is in Toronto. Instead of encouraging these types of events for community building and for children's safety, the city instead treats the idea as an inconvenience. We can look to the success of the Church Street pedestrianization initiative this past summer, and Halloween annually, for validation that it is, in fact, not the end of the world to close a street, let alone a local road, but other cities are light years ahead in creating spaces that let kids be kids. Last year, 74 schools and childcare centres in New York City opted to close their adjacent street to traffic so children could play [under their School Streets program](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/the-right-of-play-expanding-school-streets-access-in-nyc/), while Seattle has about 20 miles of semi-pedestrianised local roads dubbed ["Healthy Streets"](https://www.seattle.gov/transportation/projects-and-programs/programs/healthy-streets) with traffic calming that can easily be closed for events, no permit required, or for kids to setup a basketball game for example. While the debate in these two cities is that these pandemic-era programs could be even bolder, Toronto has nothing: no easy way to set up neighbourhood events, Quiet Streets and ActiveTO were scrapped, and our provincial government is currently ripping out the bike lanes that provide safe ways for children to cycle.

Do we refrain from these initiatives because Toronto's political machine is so afraid of being labelled as "anti-car"? Is it because the city tends to be overly risk-adverse to lawsuits and liability on the off chance something happens? Or perhaps because there is no one bold enough to champion such a program? It can be all three, but it comes down to a matter of priorities. It is not children, Vision Zero, nor community cohesion at the top of the list but rather preserving the status quo and formulating policy around the benefactors of car-centric neighbourhood planning: drivers.
