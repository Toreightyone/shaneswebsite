*Originally submiited to The Toronto Star on July 13, 2023*

It is no secret that rental prices and housing costs have skyrocketed over the past few years. A stable place to live is increasingly out of reach for those most vulnerable to housing insecurity such as first-time homebuyers, students, newer arrivals to Canada, and precariously employed workers. Every city has a part to play in ensuring housing is available, but Mississauga seems to be the one most apathetic to this responsibility.

Mayor Bonnie Crombie said her city's plan to create 120,000 units of housing by 2030 recognizes the need to "build up, instead of out," to help align closer to the province's goal of 1.5 million homes. Although she immediately followed up by declaring that Mississauga will not sacrifice its identity and character to meet this goal. Much of the city is regulated for low-density; building up is not something the city does with open arms, as they typically kick and scream over the type of high-rises and what heights they would allow from developers, making it incredibly difficult for new housing to get built at a time when it really should not.

In 2023 alone, two 40- and 42-storeys (1,139 units) towers were rejected in March at Port Credit GO for a lack of parking, despite being located next to a region-spanning rail line and the future Hurontario LRT terminus. 1,332 units across three towers were rejected at Hurontario Street and Eglinton Avenue in March due to shadowing concerns. Across the street in empty fields, 4,690 units across eight towers were rejected in May. A 38-storey (350 units) tower was rejected in June, also at Port Credit GO.

In fact, on Lakeshore Road in January, a 17-storey proposal was called "one of the most offensive I've seen to date," by its local councillor because of its height gradient with the building beside it.

The fact these are so frequent is frankly disturbing; an internet search would uncover many, many more. 66,097 residential units, 31,311 within apartments, have been pending approval since 1998, with only one proposal submitted in 2023 currently active. Some have no chance at approval based on precedents, and most will appeal to the Ontario Land Tribunal in a process that will add an additional year before a final decision.

The province even had to step in on May 12, 2023, and double a development's size via a ministerial zoning order. Any sort of leverage that municipalities have over developers in negotiations vanishes when the province is forced to step in, especially when lobbying the office of the Premier becomes an open-door to bypass municipal processes. City standards for developments should be more so about the percentage of affordable units or what infrastructure the developer will pay for instead of restrictive all-or-nothing hard caps on heights and feigning victim over shadows.

It is apparent that "sacrificing its identity and character," is thinly veiled doublespeak for keeping the low density, Square One-centered suburb of the eighties, resulting in an unaffordable, housing-scarce city of the twenty-twenties. According to the CMHC, two housing projects were started in May 2023, despite a target of 1,000 – a massive underperformance that, along with her comments about developing the Greenbelt, will haunt frontrunner Bonnie Crombie's Liberal Party leadership campaign. The city placed second-last among towns and cities province-wide, only edging out Ajax with zero. Although even Ajax had forty projects completed within the month, while Mississauga had one. The city's lagging housing policy will be highlighted by fellow candidates as nimbyist, and one that should not be replicated province-wide, especially if Mississauga does not make a reversal before December.

The pending legalization of duplexes, triplexes, townhomes, garden suites, and midrise apartments citywide is a great first step. Although low-gain conversions by homeowners alone will not solve the crisis. Midrise apartments, or taller, should be considered along most major arteries and transit corridors.

The goal is not to recreate downtown Toronto everywhere in Mississauga, but meaningful densification must happen next to current and future transit lines like the Hurontario LRT and the Lakeshore West line, something that nullifies complaints about parking and congestion. These higher-order transit lines provide the infrastructure that will support increased density, something that contradicts the rationale for cancelling projects adjacent to them. The opportunity for developments presents itself, and yet the city still thinks car-owners living in lower-unit buildings will be the demographic that maximizes the potential of not only a transit hub, but the funding the city put towards it.

This is not the first time Mississauga has refused easy access to the transit facilities they partially paid for. If you were to ride along the six-hundred-million-dollar bus-only transitway from Winston Churchill in the west to Square One to Highway 427 in the east, you would quickly see the biggest problem with the project — very few passengers board or disembark at these grandiose intermediate stations between termini, because a majority of stations were built in the middle of nowhere, and eight years later, still only serve empty fields. Dixie station's closest house is 100m away but requires a 1km walk. Tomken station's closest is 150m away but requires 800m of walking. Cawthra station is the worst of the bunch with its closest house 200m away and yet pedestrians must travel 1.7km to get to it. These homes are also detached, single-family buildings. Cawthra and Dixie have parking lots, but it is hard to imagine a scenario where someone would go through the effort to drive to the station, board an express bus, and very likely will have to transfer to a less frequent bus at their destination station when they could have just driven the full distance.

The Mississauga Transitway was built in the Parkway Belt, an area designated for 'conservation', despite housing Highway 403, various parking lots, Hydro One facilities, and the transitway itself. Perhaps it should have been built on Burnhamthorpe Parkway as urbanist Gil Peñalosa suggested, but the reality is that governments past built this massive right-of-way where it is, and the government of today should use it to the potential that taxpayers paid for. The adjacent Eastgate Parkway cannot look like a façade of rural Ontario. The transit-orientated community should be widely adopted with higher-density housing next to these underutilised stations that provide frequent cross-city travel and connections to Toronto, Guelph, Kitchener-Waterloo, Brampton, and Hamilton. Given the stances on developing grasslands by the mayor and the premier, it should not be too difficult to negotiate zoning changes to this with coordination with Hydro One. Winston Churchill and Erin Mills stations, where the only thing in their vicinities are their own parking lots, also have built-in space for development.

Mississauga needs to get serious about the bureaucracy attached to building much needed housing and more importantly, reducing the stringency of the approval process. A CMHC report found that an increase in land use regulation results in a decrease of housing affordability — something must change, and it does not look like Mississauga will be building their own social housing any time soon. Shadows, sharp height gradients, parking, and congestion are not life or death issues, availability of housing is.
