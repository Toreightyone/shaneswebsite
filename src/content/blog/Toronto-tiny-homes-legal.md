*Originally submitted to the Toronto Star on February 18, 2025*

There is no question that the city finds itself struggling to provide the necessities of life to its homeless population. [The Auditor General found that for the 2023/2024 winter](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/au/bgrd/backgroundfile-252902.pdf), 174 individuals per night were turned away, unable to be matched to a shelter bed because there was insufficient capacity. Although the city did not collect data on how many were turned away from winter respite centres and warming centres, she found evidence that this occurred on a nightly basis at some locations. The year-round data paints a grimmer picture as Toronto Shelter and Support Services (TSSS) themselves [reports in budget documents](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2025/bu/bgrd/backgroundfile-251962.pdf) an average of 225 people per day turned away for 2024, up from 202 in 2023 and 105 in 2022, and demand far exceeding capacity for years to come.

Although Mayor Chow earmarked an additional \$51 million towards TSSS in her 2025 budget, a lot more than an 8.5% increase is required to provide enough beds to match the overwhelming demand for shelter services. Each person rejected from entering the already indignifying shelter system means an additional person sleeps on the sidewalk, in an alleyway, in an apartment lobby, on a TTC vehicle, in a library, in a ravine, or in a tent in the park – not out of selfishness or public disorder, but out of necessity.

To save the day comes 'Toronto the Good' and [Ryan Donais](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/toronto-tiny-mobile-homes-1.7419805). He saw the horrific situation that homeless individuals face: not knowing where their next meal comes from, where they will sleep, or whether their possessions will be stolen in the night. With the help from donations from residents, he set up modular homes to provide safety, shelter, and warmth – and the city wanted to stomp it out, [sending him a cease and desist](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/tiny-tiny-homes-cease-and-desist-toronto-1.7458835) within days of two double-digit-centimetre snowstorms no less. This is not unlike during the pandemic when the city applied for a court injunction against [Khaleel Seivwright](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/ont-homeless-encampments-1.5920822), who built wooden shelters with his own time and effort because of how untenable the city's handling of homelessness became.

Why are we not embracing the work and philanthropy of private citizens to make up where city efforts have failed? When we make our rounds to Ottawa or Queen's Park begging for additional funds for shelter space, what message does it send when we reject free help. It is worth noting that [in July 2024, Toronto City Council asked the province](https://secure.toronto.ca/council/agenda-item.do?item=2024.EX16.5) to allow for the millions of dollars in private donations to go towards preservation of the Ontario Science Centre – why is this any different?

Unlike Toronto, [Waterloo](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/kitchener-waterloo/waterloo-region-outdoor-shelter-tiny-homes-1.6797465) and [Peterborough](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/peterborough-modular-housing-project-unhoused-people-1.7138608) have worked with community organisations to manage these spaces safely, acknowledging that they need all the help they can get, both housing 50 people. [Kelowna, BC](https://www.kelownanow.com/watercooler/news/news/Kelowna/City_of_Kelowna_announces_operator_of_3rd_tiny_home_site_for_the_homeless/) will be housing 180 by mid-2025. [Saint John, NB](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/new-brunswick/sunnyside-tiny-home-project-united-way-saint-john-council-1.7455487) will be housing up to 80. However, [Finland's solution](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/sunday/the-sunday-edition-for-january-26-2020-1.5429251/housing-is-a-human-right-how-finland-is-eradicating-homelessness-1.5437402) is one we should ultimately strive for. By providing unconditional housing for those without, healthcare and policing costs are lowered, the number of taxpayers and tax revenue is increased to the point of actually being a net gain, and treating other issues like substance abuse becomes much easier.

Instead, Toronto seems more interested in caving to the demands of those whose purpose is to push the homeless out of sight. If not tiny homes on city-owned public land, then what and where? An apartment-style complex in the suburbs? Too bad the city councillor for Willowdale and other resident groups have delayed the modular housing project at 175 Cummer Avenue for years. Ignoring the problem exacerbates the issue of encampments; increases death from overdoses, the cold, and accidental fires; punts our homelessness response to our libraries, TTC, and justice system to solve; and is fundamentally antithesis to both the city's own messaging but their human rights obligations.
